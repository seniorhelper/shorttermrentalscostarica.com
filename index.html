The Field Notes

Short-term rentals in Costa Rica: what 2026 actually looks like

Whether you're booking a week in paradise or weighing a property that pays for itself, the same forces shape your experience: location, seasonality, supply, and a tax landscape that just changed. Here's the honest read.

For travelers: book the right coast, not just the right house

Costa Rica isn't one destination — it's half a dozen, each with its own rhythm. Guanacaste's Gold Coast (Tamarindo, Flamingo, Playa Grande) delivers reliable dry-season sun and the most polished villa inventory, which is why nightly rates run highest there. The Nicoya Peninsula — Nosara and Santa Teresa — trades polish for soul: surf breaks, yoga, and open-air bungalows inside one of the planet's Blue Zones. The South Pacific around Uvita and Dominical is greener, wilder, and a better value per bedroom. The Southern Caribbean near Puerto Viejo is the most affordable and the most laid-back. And San José, while nobody's beach dream, is the practical base for business trips and airport-adjacent first nights.

A booking tip that saves real money: the dry season (roughly December through April) is peak, and rates climb accordingly. The green season carries afternoon rain but also lower prices, thinner crowds, and a landscape that's actually green. If your dates are flexible, the shoulder months of May and November are the sweet spot — close to dry-season weather at green-season prices.

Location decides more about your return than anything you'll ever buy for the property itself.

For investors: the numbers are real, but so is the gap between markets

Costa Rica is one of Central America's most developed short-term-rental markets, with roughly 41,500 active listings in the first half of 2026. The national average host earns around $1,700 a month — but that average hides a canyon. A Tamarindo villa averaging $357 a night at high occupancy can clear $37,000 a year, while a San José apartment at $59 a night earns its keep through steadier, business-driven occupancy closer to 53–57%. The lesson isn't “buy the most expensive coast.” It's that gross revenue, nightly rate, and occupancy each tell a different part of the story, and the best investment balances all three against the purchase price.

Watch supply as closely as demand. Parts of central Jacó (over 4,000 listings), Tamarindo proper, and pockets of Nosara are showing oversaturation — inventory climbing while occupancy slips. In a crowded market, the top operators still win, but they win on execution: better photography, dynamic pricing, faster guest response. The data is blunt about it — the best hosts run 10 to 20 percentage points above average occupancy purely on how they manage the listing.

The 2026 rules every host has to know

Short-term renting is fully legal and regulated nationwide under the “hospedaje no tradicional” framework (Law 9742 and Decreto 43154-H-TUR). But legal means compliant, and compliance has two separate doors. First, you register your rental activity with the Costa Rican Tourism Institute (ICT). Second — and entirely separately — you register as a taxpayer with the Ministry of Finance (Hacienda) and issue electronic invoices. You can be right with one and wrong with the other; both are required, and operating unregistered risks fines.

On tax, two numbers matter. Stays under 30 days are a taxable service, so you charge guests 13% VAT (IVA) and remit it monthly — you collect it, you don't keep it. Then the headline change: beginning at the end of 2026, platforms like Airbnb and Vrbo will withhold a 12.75% tax on gross platform rental income before the money reaches you. “Gross” is the key word — it's calculated before your expenses, which is why hosts who run lean and document everything are scrambling to choose the most advantageous tax regime now rather than after the withholding goes live. Many municipalities add a local tax on top. None of this makes Costa Rica a bad STR market; it just means the back-of-napkin math needs to happen before you buy, not after.

Booking platforms aren't all the same

For travelers, Airbnb has the deepest Costa Rica inventory and the widest range, from $40 surf hostels to $1,000-a-night cliffside villas. Vrbo skews toward whole-home rentals, which suits families and groups who don't want to share. Booking.com blends hotels and rentals with flexible cancellation that's handy when your plans are still loose. For hosts, listing across more than one platform widens your funnel — but remember each one feeds the same tax reporting, so “spreading out” doesn't spread out your obligations. The smart move is to pick the platforms that match your property type and guest persona, then manage them with consistent pricing and instant responses.

What separates a $1,700 month from a $6,000 one

The national average host earns about $1,700 a month, yet beach villas with pools routinely clear three or four times that in high season. Some of that gap is location and property, but a surprising amount is pure operating skill. The data consistently shows top-performing hosts running 10 to 20 percentage points above their market's average occupancy — and they're not doing it with magic. They're doing it with photography that sells the lifestyle in the first three frames, dynamic pricing that pushes rates up during Christmas, Easter, and US holiday weekends while filling the slow weeks at a discount, and response times measured in minutes rather than hours, because the platforms reward fast hosts with better placement.

The property itself matters more in the tropics than most first-time investors expect. A pool is close to mandatory at the higher price tiers — it's the single most-searched amenity in beach markets. Reliable air conditioning, strong Wi-Fi for the remote-work crowd, and genuine protection against humidity and salt-air corrosion separate the listings that earn five-star reviews from the ones that quietly bleed refunds. Costa Rica's climate is hard on buildings: coastal homes need more frequent painting, better drainage, and durable finishes, and the maintenance reserve that supports all of that should be a line in your pro forma from day one, not a surprise in year two.

Then there's management. You can self-manage from abroad — plenty of owners do — but the practical reality is that turnover cleaning, guest communication, maintenance calls, and tax filing all happen on Costa Rica time, in Spanish, on the ground. A good local property manager typically takes 18 to 25% of revenue, which feels steep until you price in the occupancy and review-score lift a professional operation delivers, plus the compliance headaches it absorbs. For a remote owner, the right manager is often the difference between a passive asset and a second job in another country.

Reading occupancy without fooling yourself

Occupancy is the number that lies most easily. A 56% annual figure in Tamarindo sounds modest next to a city market's 70%, but it's earned at a $181-to-$357 nightly rate, which is why the revenue still lands near $37,000. San José's higher occupancy comes at a $59 rate, so the two markets meet in very different places on the income line. The trap for new investors is anchoring on a single metric — chasing the highest occupancy, or the highest nightly rate, instead of the combination that actually produces revenue against the property's purchase price. Always run the full triangle: rate, occupancy, and what you paid to get in.

A traveler's cheat sheet to the regions

If you're booking rather than buying, here's the shorthand. Choose Guanacaste for guaranteed dry-season sun, calm swimmable beaches, and the easiest infrastructure — it's the most resort-like coast, ideal for first-timers and families. Choose the Nicoya Peninsula if you want surf and a wellness-minded, barefoot-luxury vibe, and you don't mind rougher roads to get there. Choose the South Pacific if you're after rainforest-meets-ocean drama, waterfalls, and whale-watching, with more house for your money. Choose the Southern Caribbean for culture, reggae-tinged nightlife, and the lowest prices, accepting that it rains year-round and the pace is genuinely slow. And keep San José in mind for a single airport-night bookend rather than a destination in itself.

One last booking habit worth building: read the reviews for what isn't in the photos. Road access, water pressure, generator backup during outages, real distance to the beach (“walkable” can mean a sweaty 20 minutes), and how responsive the host actually is when something breaks. The listings that earn consistent five stars in Costa Rica tend to be honest about the tropics rather than airbrushing them — and an honest host is the best predictor of a smooth trip.

Getting started as a host, step by step

If you've decided to host, the path is more orderly than the forums make it sound. Start by validating the specific property against real market data — not the region average, but comparable listings on your exact street, in your exact size class, and their actual booked calendars. That tells you the realistic revenue band before you commit. Next, build the compliance foundation: register your activity with the ICT, register as a taxpayer with Hacienda, and set up electronic invoicing. Doing this first means you're in the system cleanly when the 2026 platform reporting goes live, rather than backfilling under pressure.

With the legal base set, turn to the listing itself. Professional photography pays for itself faster than almost any other dollar you'll spend — guests decide in seconds, and the cover image alone moves your booking rate. Write a description that's specific about what the place is and honest about what it isn't, set a dynamic pricing strategy that breathes with the seasons, and decide which platforms fit your guest persona. Then nail the operational basics: a reliable cleaning turnover, a local contact for emergencies, and a response time that keeps you in the platforms' good graces. Most hosts who struggle aren't in bad markets — they're under-managing good ones.

The short version

Costa Rica rewards people who do their homework on either side of the door. Travelers who match the coast to their trip — and time it to the season — get more paradise for less money. Investors who pick the market on data, price the property against its real revenue, and get compliant before the 2026 withholding lands end up with an asset that genuinely pays. The mistakes happen when people skip the numbers and trust the postcard.

  • Match the region to your trip — or your guest persona.
  • Shoulder season (May, November) is the value sweet spot.
  • Register with both ICT and Hacienda before you host.
  • Budget for 13% VAT and the 12.75% platform withholding.
  • In saturated markets, execution beats inventory every time.
